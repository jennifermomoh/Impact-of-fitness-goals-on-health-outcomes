Outcomes: weight, blood pressure, heart rate, sleep scores

Hypotheses:
1. Goal-setting increases motivation and adherence, leading to improved outcomes.
2. Users will show higher activity levels and consistency post-goal setting.


Methods:
- non experimental

- Health Metrics

Strengthen the analysis

Add statistical tests — your findings need p-values and effect sizes (Cohen's d) to be credible, especially for the sales use case. At minimum: paired t-test (pre vs post within goal-setters) and independent t-test (goal-setters vs control post-period).
Difference-in-differences — compare the change in goal-setters against the change in controls, not just absolute values. This controls for natural trends and is more defensible.
Adherence over time — does the improvement hold through week 12 or fade after week 4? This is critical for the sales pitch (sustained vs temporary change).
Segmentation worth adding

Which goal type (steps / calories / active minutes / distance) produces the best health outcomes?
Does age group or fitness level moderate the effect? (e.g., do low-fitness users benefit more?)
Do users who hit their goal more often show better outcomes? (dose-response)
Gaps to flag

Blood pressure is listed as a key outcome but isn't in the dataset — worth noting as a limitation
Your method is better described as quasi-experimental (you have a comparison group), not non-experimental — this actually strengthens your credibility
Selection bias — goal-setters may already be more motivated; worth acknowledging as a caveat
For the sales team specifically

Lead with a headline number: "Goal-setters walked X,000 more steps/day and reduced resting HR by X bpm"
Include confidence intervals alongside means
A before/after chart per metric is more compelling than tables for a sales deck
